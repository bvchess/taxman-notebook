## 0. Summary

*A second round (Sections 10 to 14) puts game 5061 first, and a third (Section 15, after E10 to E14) gives the proof of 5061 as a finite computation by E12's gluing lemma, refereed there, and sizes it.  The second round sharpens items 3 to 5 below: the 16P theorem can be generated by a program, the exchange law reduces to a one-board statement (T), and the 21P exceptions are one regime of the family, read in game n − 1.*

1. **The parity bound's exchange formula (Theorem A, proved, no conjecture).**  For odd n whose vector lies in the
   span of the sources' vectors of board n − 1, PB(n) = PB(n − 1) + n − x_G, where x_G is the smallest member of the
   unique set of greedy-basis sources whose vectors sum to v(n); given (♦), x_G is the shadow x of every exchange step.
   So at every exchange δ_PB(n) = x, and the exchange law δ(n) = x is exactly the statement that the gap does not
   change: rise(n) = 0.  Its open half, δ(n) ≥ x, is "the gap cannot fall at an exchange".  The same formula with J3
   proves rise = 0 at every odd semiprime from 189 on, and the prime step proves it at every prime; given U at n − 1
   and (♦), rise = 0 at every half-out game.  **So the gap changes only at relaxations and restrictions**, and at an
   exchange only if the exchange law fails (never seen: Section 3.4).
2. **The parity bound has the frame model's three kinds of step (proved).**  Write PB(n) = ΣG(n) + Q\*(n), G the
   greedy basis of the sources' vectors and Q\* the heaviest set of numbers up to n/2 independent at both parities
   together with G.  At a restriction Q\* can only fall and δ_PB is its fall; at a relaxation s = n/2 joins the
   numbers up to n/2 and δ_PB = s − (Q\*(n) − Q\*(n − 1)); at an exchange Q\* does not change.  This is P\* with the
   playable promotion sets replaced by the parity-feasible ones, line for line (S 2.2), and given U and (♦) the gap
   is Q\*(n) − P\*(n): the whole gap lives in the promotion problem.
3. **The clean-window principle, stated precisely (Section 2) and measured (Section 4).**  The right hypothesis is
   not depth 2 but the depth of the cascade.  With the window clean to depth 3, rise = 0 at every one of the 126
   relaxations and restrictions of 187..5060 (6.1% of the chain's CPU) and at 234 of the 238 past 5060, where the
   four exceptions are in the coordinator's flag list (three have a large positive rise at 21P and 30P, a short
   best-known at n; 9140 has rise −1).  At depth 2 the inequality rise ≥ 0 holds at all 182 proven games and the
   equality fails once, at 2512 = 16·157, which is clean to depth 2 while the 16P cascade ends in column 3; there the
   parity bound takes a route the clean columns do not offer, and underprices the room for n by 98.  So: **the
   statement to prove is the equality rise = 0 under "clean to the cascade's depth"; the statement to use is the
   inequality gap(n) ≥ gap(n − 1), since a game at opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1) settles n under it and nothing
   under the equality.**  Neither is proved here; Section 4.4 says what a proof needs and why the known technique
   (L1 to L4) does not reach it as it stands.
4. **The cascade principle (measured, and the shape of every family law).**  In a clean window the change from n − 1
   to n is an alternating path along the columns through the hubs: n takes a hub from the cell of the next column,
   that column's cell falls back inside its column, the member it frees takes the next hub from the column below, and
   so on; the pattern of a family is the list of cells the path passes.  The families 16, 20, 24 and 40 (Section 5)
   are single such paths, of depths 3, 2, 2 and 1 (40P's runs inside column 1 through layer 5: 40P₁ → 8P₁ → 24P₁ →
   12P₁ → 36P₁ → 18P₁).  A proof in the style of L2 and L4 is a case analysis over one finite configuration per
   cofactor; this report specifies it (Section 5.3) and does not carry it out by hand.
5. **The exchange law (Section 6).**  With Theorem A the law is the equality of two promotion optima over one
   parity-feasible family (P\*(n) = P\*(n − 1) while Q\* is the same), so the property behind it is that no odd core
   through n is heavier than every odd core through x: a global statement, as E1 found.  The E1 detours are
   consistent with Theorem A, and Section 6.3 states the sharpest testable form found.
6. **The gap (Section 7).**  It is a running sum of defects created and dissolved at relaxations and restrictions; it
   never moves at primes, semiprimes, half-out games (given U) or, if the exchange law holds, exchanges.  In clean
   windows the defects of n − 1 survive n's arrival because the arrival's cascade is a GF(2) circuit the bound prices
   exactly; where the window is unclean the cascade can meet a core that only one of the two models sees, in either
   direction.

## 1. Words

The game, playable sets, instances, lists, payment schemes, layers, sources, U\*, the parity vectors, PB, the gap,
δ, δ_PB and rise are S's (S 1.1 to 1.4), and the three kinds of searched step S 2.2's.  v(s) is the incidence vector
of s over GF(2) (ones at its maximal factors), e_s the unit vector of s ≤ n/2.  Ω(x) is the number of prime factors
of x with multiplicity.

* **Columns and cells (B 1, made precise).**  P = P₀ is the largest prime factor of n, c = n/P its cofactor, and
  P₁ > P₂ > … the primes below P.  A number w whose largest prime factor is P_j is the *cell* (k, j) with k = w/P_j,
  in *row* k and *column* j.  Column j of board m is the set of its cells (k, j) ≤ m: the rows it holds are the k
  with kP_j ≤ m, since for P_j > c every k ≤ c has its primes below P_j.  Column 0 of board n holds exactly the rows
  1..c.
* **Clean (B 1, the definition this report uses).**  The window of n is *clean to depth d* when (c + 1)P_i > n for
  every 1 ≤ i ≤ d.  Then columns 1..d of board n, and of board n − 1, hold exactly the rows 1..c, as column 0 does.
  The depth is computed from n's factorization alone (`patterns.clean_depth`).  Cleanliness to depth d says nothing
  about columns beyond d, and nothing about the hubs.
* **Hubs.**  A cell (k, j) has the maximal factors (k/q, j) for the primes q dividing k, which lie in its column, and
  the number k itself, the *hub* of row k, which every cell of row k lists.  A hub k ≤ c lies in every column's
  row-k cell's list, in clean and unclean columns alike, and it is listed by numbers that are no cell of the window
  (hub 12 by 12t for every prime t ≤ n/12).  In a payment scheme a hub pays for at most one member, so the hubs are
  the only resource the columns share; the column factors (k/q, j) are private to their column.
* **The change and the pattern (B 1).**  D is the set of moves of the recorded optimal set of n − 1 that the recorded
  optimal set of n gives up, A those it takes up besides n; δ(n) = ΣD − ΣA.  When every member of D and A is a cell
  (k, j) with |j| ≤ 12, the pair (D, A) in cell labels is the *pattern*, a formula in P₀, P₁, …  The *depth of a
  pattern* is the largest column index it uses; the *depth of a family* is the depth of its commonest pattern on
  187..5060 (`study/depth_check.py` prints them: 8 → 1, 12 → 1, 16 → 3, 20 → 2, 24 → 2, 40 → 1).
* **G(m)**, the greedy basis of the sources' vectors of board m: the sources kept when they are taken by decreasing
  value and each is kept whose vector is not in the span of those kept before it ([the greedy-basis proposition
  W3](https://github.com/bvchess/taxman/blob/287486e/docs/theory/parity-bound.md#the-greedy-basis-proposition)).  G(m) = U\*(m) exactly when (♦) holds in game
  m (W3), which is checked to 100,000.
* **Q\*(m)**, the parity bound's promotion sum: the largest ΣL over sets L of numbers of 2..⌊m/2⌋ such that G(m) ∪ L
  is independent at both parities.  By W3, PB(m) = ΣG(m) + Q\*(m) (every heaviest set holds exactly G(m) among the
  sources, so the maximum over heaviest sets is the maximum over their lower parts).

## 2. The principle, precisely

Three statements, from the one the data show most sharply to the one a proof would establish.

**(P1) The parity form.**  For n ≥ 187 a relaxation or restriction whose window is clean to the depth of its cascade,
gap(n) = gap(n − 1): opt(n) = opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1).  *Measured* (Section 4): exact at every game of the
chain clean to depth 3, and at depth 2 except 2512.  What "the cascade's depth" is, without the recorded sets, is
the weak point; the uniform "depth 3" is what the data support for the cofactors seen (up to 25).

**(P2) The inequality.**  Under the same hypothesis gap(n) ≥ gap(n − 1), that is opt(n) ≤ opt(n − 1) + PB(n) −
PB(n − 1).  This is what settles a game: with it, a game of n scoring opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1) is optimal, and
PB takes seconds.  *Measured*: no proven counterexample at depth ≥ 2 (182 games); at depth 1, 22 of 156 chain games
break it, so depth 1 is not a hypothesis.

**(P3) The cascade (the mechanism, and the form a proof takes).**  In a window clean to depth d, every optimal set
of n is, outside n and the cells of columns 0..d, an optimal set of n − 1 with the same payments, and inside them
the two differ by an alternating path: n pays with its half or a hub; the cell that paid with that hub falls back on
a factor of its own column; the member that factor paid for, if it loses its payment, either leaves (a loss) or takes
a hub from the column below; and so on, ending at a loss.  δ(n) is the value of the cheapest such path that keeps
every layer instance acyclic.  *Measured* in the sense that the recorded changes of the clean games of the families
8, 12, 16, 20, 24 and 40 are such paths (450 of 450, B 4.3); *proved* for 8 and 12 (L2, L4), where the path has one
step.  P3 is the finite problem of Section 5.3.

Why P1 and P3 are one principle (B 2, sharpened): an alternating path of hub transfers is, over GF(2), a circuit of
the parity matroid in which n sits, the members the path drops and adds being exactly a matroid-intersection
augmenting path from n.  The parity bound prices that path exactly; it misprices only a core that is a circuit in one
model and not the other, an odd core.  A clean column has no odd core among the cells that a path can touch (its
cells' private factors give the re-payments of L1 their "factor of its own"), so the two models take the same path;
an unclean column can hold an odd core (9r at 184, 15r at 204, the rows 17 and 18 of column 3 at 2512), and then the
models can part, in either direction.

## 3. What is proved

### 3.1 The parity bound's exchange formula

**Theorem A (proved; no U, no (♦)).**  Let n ≥ 3 be odd, and let V be the span over GF(2) of the vectors v(s) of
the sources s of board n − 1.

1. If v(n) ∉ V, then G(n) = G(n − 1) + n.
2. If v(n) ∈ V, there is exactly one set C ⊆ G(n − 1) with Σ_{c ∈ C} v(c) = v(n); let x_G be its smallest member.
   Then G(n) = G(n − 1) − x_G + n, G(n) and G(n − 1) have the same span at each parity, Q\*(n) = Q\*(n − 1), and

       PB(n) = PB(n − 1) + n − x_G,   that is   δ_PB(n) = x_G.

*Proof.*  On boards n − 1 and n the numbers up to n/2 are the same, since n is odd, so every number but n has the
same vectors on both, and the sources of board n are those of board n − 1 and n.  The greedy algorithm on board n
keeps n first (v(n) ≠ 0) and then takes the old sources in the old order.  Write K_y for the sources of board n − 1
above y that G(n − 1) keeps.

*Part 1.*  Suppose, by induction down the sources, that board n keeps n and exactly K_y above y.  Board n keeps y iff
v(y) ∉ span(K_y + n).  If y ∈ G(n − 1) and v(y) ∈ span(K_y + n), then v(n) ∈ span(K_y + y) ⊆ V, against the
hypothesis; so y is kept.  If y ∉ G(n − 1), v(y) ∈ span(K_y) ⊆ span(K_y + n), and y is rejected.

*Part 2.*  C exists since G(n − 1) spans V ([the greedy algorithm](https://github.com/bvchess/taxman/blob/287486e/docs/theory/primer/greedy.md#the-greedy-algorithm)
keeps a basis of the span), and is unique since G(n − 1) is independent.  The vectors of different layers lie on
disjoint coordinates (the maximal factors of a number of layer k have layer k − 1), so every member of C has n's
layer.  Down the sources: for y > x_G in G(n − 1), v(y) ∈ span(K_y + n) would give v(n) ∈ span(K_y + y), a
representation of v(n) by members of G(n − 1) all above x_G, against uniqueness; so y is kept, and a rejected y
stays rejected as in part 1.  At y = x_G: v(x_G) = v(n) + Σ_{C − x_G} v(c) ∈ span(K_{x_G} + n), so x_G is rejected.
For y < x_G: the kept set above y on board n is K_y − x_G + n, whose span equals span(K_y): it contains v(n) =
Σ_C v(c) with C ⊆ K_y, and conversely v(x_G) = v(n) + Σ_{C − x_G} v(c); so y's decision is the same on both
boards.  Hence G(n) = G(n − 1) − x_G + n.

*The spans at each parity.*  At the parity of n's layer, n and x_G have extra coordinates and the other members
contribute the same vectors on both boards.  At the other parity, G(n − 1) contributes v(x_G) and the vectors of its
other members, G(n) contributes v(n) and the same others, and the two spans are equal by the two identities above.
A set L of numbers up to n/2 is independent at parity i together with G exactly when the classes of its parity-i
vectors modulo the span of G's parity-i vectors are independent (the primer), so the same sets L are admissible for
G(n) and for G(n − 1): Q\*(n) = Q\*(n − 1).  With PB = ΣG + Q\* (Section 1), PB(n) = PB(n − 1) − x_G + n.  ∎

**Corollary A1 (the exchange step; proved given (♦) at n − 1 and n).**  At every exchange step, U\*(n) =
U\*(n − 1) − x + n with x the shadow (measured) and δ_PB(n) = x.  So the exchange law δ(n) = x holds at n if and
only if rise(n) = 0, and its open half δ(n) ≥ x is gap(n) ≥ gap(n − 1).  Without (♦) the same holds with x_G, which
descending greedy's twin, the greedy basis, computes in the same time.  *Checked*: rise = 0 at all 103 exchange rows
of settle.csv, searched and repeats (`study/rise_kinds_out.txt`).

**Corollary A2 (semiprimes and primes; proved).**  For odd n ≥ 189 with two prime factors, rise(n) = 0: J3 gives
δ(n) = x, the smallest other member of the cycle n closes with U\*₂(n − 1) in G_n, and in layer 2 the greedy basis is
U\*₂ and the circuit of v(n) is that cycle, so x_G = x.  At a prime p, rise(p) = 0 by the prime step of PB and N5.
*Checked*: rise = 0 at all 971 odd semiprimes from 189 and all 676 primes of the chain.

**Corollary A3 (half-out games; proved given U at n − 1 and (♦) at n − 1).**  Let n be even with n/2 ∉ G(n − 1).
Then δ_PB(n) = 0, and given U at n − 1 (which with (♦) makes n/2 ∉ U\*(n − 1) the half lemma's hypothesis) δ(n) = 0,
so rise(n) = 0.  *Proof.*  No number of board n − 1 lists n/2 (its next multiple is n), so on board n no vector of a
member of a heaviest set I of board n − 1 has a one at the coordinate n/2, while v(n) has one there; so I + n is
independent at both parities on board n (at the other parity n has an extra coordinate), and PB(n) ≥ PB(n − 1) + n,
with equality by the parity ceiling.  ∎  *Checked*: rise = 0 at all 1,141 half-out games of the chain.

### 3.2 The three kinds of step in the parity bound

**Proposition B (proved).**  Let n ≥ 3.
1. *Odd n, restriction (v(n) ∉ V).*  G(n) = G(n − 1) + n, every L admissible for G(n) is admissible for G(n − 1),
   and δ_PB(n) = Q\*(n − 1) − Q\*(n) ≥ 0.
2. *Odd n, exchange (v(n) ∈ V).*  Theorem A.
3. *Even n, s = n/2.*  G(n) = G(n − 1) − s + n when s ∈ G(n − 1) (relaxation), and G(n − 1) + n when s ∉ G(n − 1)
   (half-out, A3).  In a relaxation s joins the numbers up to n/2, with e_s at the parity of its layer in place of its
   extra coordinate, every L admissible for G(n − 1) is admissible for G(n) (v(n) has a one at s that no other vector
   has), and δ_PB(n) = s − (Q\*(n) − Q\*(n − 1)) ∈ [0, s].

*Proof.*  Part 1 is part 1 of Theorem A with the span argument: adding n to the fixed part can only shrink the
admissible family.  Part 3: n is kept first; an old source y ≠ s is decided as on board n − 1, since v(n) has a one
at the coordinate s that no old source's vector has, so v(n) ∉ span of any set of old vectors and the argument of
Theorem A, part 1, applies; s is no source of board n.  The rest is the definitions.  ∎

This is S 2.2's table with Prom replaced by the parity-feasible family ℒ and P\* by Q\*.  Given U at n and (♦) at n,
opt(n) = ΣU\*(n) + P\*(n) (X8) and PB(n) = ΣU\*(n) + Q\*(n), so

    gap(n) = Q\*(n) − P\*(n),

and rise(n) = [Q\*(n) − Q\*(n − 1)] − [P\*(n) − P\*(n − 1)]: at a restriction, the parity relaxation's fall less the
game's; at a relaxation, the parity relaxation's gain less the game's.  The whole gap is the promotion problem's.

### 3.3 Two one-line lemmas that say what a proof of the parity form needs

Let n be a relaxation or restriction, T′ an optimal set of n − 1 and S\* an optimal set of n with S\* = T′ − D + A + n.

**Lemma C (the transfer lemma; proved).**  If some heaviest set I of board n − 1 independent at both parities has
D ⊆ I, A ∩ I = ∅, and I − D + A + n independent at both parities on board n, then rise(n) ≥ 0.  *Proof.*  PB(n) ≥
Σ(I − D + A + n) = PB(n − 1) + n − δ(n), so δ_PB(n) ≤ δ(n).  ∎

**Lemma D (the game lemma; proved).**  If some heaviest set I of board n − 1 and a heaviest set J of board n with
n ∈ J have J = I − D_Q + A_Q + n, and T′ − D_Q + A_Q + n is playable on board n, then rise(n) ≤ 0; a game of n
scoring opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1) proves the same.  *Proof.*  opt(n) ≥ ΣT′ − ΣD_Q + ΣA_Q + n = opt(n − 1) +
PB(n) − PB(n − 1).  ∎

So rise ≤ 0 is always certified by a game, and the proof direction is rise ≥ 0: that the game's change (D, A) can be
applied to a parity-optimal set.  In a window clean to the cascade's depth the measured (D, A) lies in the window's
cells; what Lemma C then needs is that a parity-optimal set of n − 1 agrees with the game's on the window and that the
cascade's independence on board n is decided inside the window plus the hubs.  Section 4.4 says where that fails.

### 3.4 What this settles about the gap, before any conjecture

Combining A1 to A3 with Proposition B: **the gap of the chain changes only at relaxations and restrictions**, and at
an exchange only where the exchange law fails, which the chain never shows (B 4.5's 264 searched exchanges, and here
all 103 exchange rows).  This is the proved part of B 4.5's judgment and of Section 7.

### 4.3 Which statement is right

* **The equality rise = 0, under "clean to the ladder's depth", is the statement the mechanism predicts** (Section 2,
  P1): the two models take the same augmenting path.  Measured: no proven exception; among best-known games only the
  four flagged ones at depth ≥ 3.  Its weakness is the hypothesis: the ladder's depth depends on the cofactor (1 for
  8, 12 and 40; 2 for 20 and 24; 3 for 16; more for 30, 32, 36), so a rule needs a table d(c), proved cofactor by
  cofactor.  "Depth 3" is a uniform hypothesis that fits every cofactor the chain shows clean (8 to 25).
* **The inequality gap(n) ≥ gap(n − 1) is the statement to use**: with it, a game of n scoring opt(n − 1) + PB(n) −
  PB(n − 1) is optimal, a check of seconds.  It is not a separate principle with a proof of its own: the gap rises when
  n's arrival creates an odd core (2512) and falls when it dissolves one (the 20 ceiling games of the chain where it
  falls, all relaxations or restrictions at depth 0 or 1), and both come from configurations outside the clean
  window.  So it should carry the same hypothesis as the equality; at depth 2 it has no proven counterexample, and the
  32P and 36P plays are its test.
* **Something else.**  The brief's "if the gap cannot fall then opt(n) ≤ opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1)" is one
  statement read twice: gap(n) ≥ gap(n − 1) *is* opt(n) ≤ opt(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1).  The content is in proving
  the inequality under cleanliness, and that is as hard as proving the equality.

**The rule, as of now (unproven, for the claim past 5060).**  For a relaxation or restriction n with window clean to
depth 3 and n − 1 proven: if bk(n) = bk(n − 1) + PB(n) − PB(n − 1), then n is optimal under the conjecture P2; if
bk(n) is below that, play n (its target is known to the point); if above, play n − 1.  It covers 238 games past
5060, 234 at the target now, of which 86 are beyond 8P and 12P.  At depth 2 it covers 326, 301 at the target.  Until
P2 is proved these games are "optimal if n − 1 is, given P2", a consistency check and a flag, not a proof.

### 4.4 What a proof needs, and where the known technique stops

Lemma C reduces rise ≥ 0 to applying the game's change (D, A) to a parity-optimal set I of n − 1.  In a clean window
the sources of the window agree in both models (I ∩ sources = G(n − 1) = U\*(n − 1) given (♦); the game's optimal set
holds U\*(n − 1) given U), so the ladder's sources (16P₁, 12P_j, 10P_j, …) are common; what may differ are the
promotions, the cells up to n/2 (8P₁, 6P_j, 4P_j), and these are the rungs.  In the parity bound a column's hub use
is a *hub combination*: in a clean column the four layer-3 sources span e_{2P_j} + e_9 + e_10 + e_15 and e_{3P_j} +
e_9, so 4P_j joins the set iff e_4 + e_9 + e_10 + e_15 is not already in the span, and 6P_j iff e_6 + e_10 + e_15 is
not; clean columns offer the same two combinations, so across them at most one 4P_j and one 6P_j can be in I, which
is the game's "hub 4 pays once, hub 6 pays once".  So in clean columns the parity bound's ladder is the game's, and the
greedy assignment (the heaviest cell that needs a hub gets it) is forced in both.  A proof of P1 for a cofactor is
then the family's proof (Section 5.2) written twice, once with schemes and once with spans, and the two are the same
case analysis; the parity form has no proof cheaper than the family laws, only a wider reach (any cofactor whose
ladder stands in clean columns, with one computation instead of one theorem each).

What stops a general proof is the coupling through the far hubs: a window cell like 8P₁ lists the hub 8, held far
away (by 8·q(n/8)), in both models; a cascade that reaches a far hub leaves the window, and cleanliness says nothing
about where it lands.  In the measured clean games it never does (the far hubs never move, Section 5.1), and a lemma
that says why ("a far hub's holder is never displaced by a window cell, since the holder is heavier and has no
alternative that the window could use") would be the general piece: with it and the "no odd core in the window
instance" check of Section 5.3, P1 would follow for every cofactor by one argument.  Not proved here.

## 5. The families: cascades as hub transfers

### 5.1 What the recorded sets show (measured, `study/hubs_out.txt`)

For each game of the chain in a family from 1000 on, clean to depth ≥ 1, `study/hubs.py` takes peel's payment scheme of the
recorded optimal set of n − 1 and of n and reads off which member pays with each hub.  The picture is the same at
every clean game of a family, and it is the mechanism B 5.1 described at 1168, now seen as a ladder of hubs.

* **16P.**  In game n − 1 the hub 16 is paid by (16, 1), the hub 6 by (6, 2) and the hub 4 by (4, 3): at every one of
  the 23 chain games 1648..5008 of depth ≥ 2, and at the depth-1 games with (4, 3) replaced by the cell that holds
  hub 4 there ((4, 6) at 1136, (4, 9) at 1616, (4, 11) at 2192, a number outside the window at 1328, 3568, 3632).
  In game n the ladder has moved one column up: 16 to (16, 0) = n, 6 to (6, 1), 4 to (4, 2).  The hubs 8, 9, 10, 12
  and 2 are paid by cells of primes far above P (8 by 8·q(n/8), the heaviest 8-cell on the board) in both games and
  do not move.  So the change is three hub transfers, each one column up, and the pattern
  δ = 8P₁ + 6P₂ + 4P₃ − 6P₁ − 4P₂ is their price: column 1 loses its 8-cell (16P₁ falls back on 8P₁) and gains its
  6-cell, column 2 loses its 6-cell and gains its 4-cell, column 3 loses its 4-cell.  The cascade's depth is 3
  because the last rung of the ladder stands in column 3; where hub 4 stands elsewhere (an unclean window), the last
  loss is that holder, which is B 4.4's refinement δ = 8P₁ − 6(P₁ − P₂) − (4P₂ − h₄).
* **40P.**  Two shapes, both ending at 18P₁.  At 4280: n pays with the hub 40, 40P₁ falls back on 8P₁ (which 24P₁
  had paid with), 24P₁ on 12P₁ (which 36P₁ had paid with), 36P₁ on 18P₁, a move of layer 4, which is lost.  At
  3160: n pays with its own factor 8P (which 24P had paid with), 24P falls back on 12P (36P's), 36P on the hub 36
  (36P₁'s), 36P₁ on 18P₁, lost.  A single-column chain through the layer-5 sources 40P_j, 24P_j, 36P_j and the
  layer-4 factors 8P_j, 12P_j, 18P_j; its depth is 1, and the law δ(40P) = 18P₁ holds at 13 of 13 clean games (B

* **24P.**  n takes the hub 24 from 24P₁, which falls back on 12P₁ (a move, lost); the factor 12P₁ paid with (4P₁ or
  6P₁) is freed, and through 20P₁ or 18P₁ the cell 10P₁ or 9P₁ becomes a move and takes the hub 10 or 9 from column

Two facts of these tables matter for a proof.  (i) The far hubs (8, 9, 10, 12, 2 for 16P) stand at cells of primes
far above P, since the heaviest k-cell on the board is k·q(n/k), and they never move; what moves is the ladder of hubs
that the window's columns hold because their column factors are saturated (in a clean column the four layer-3 sources
9P_j, 10P_j, 14P_j, 15P_j use up 2P_j, 3P_j, 5P_j, 7P_j, so 4P_j and 6P_j can be moves only with the hubs 4 and 6).
(ii) The ladder is the greedy assignment of hubs to columns (the heaviest cell that needs the hub gets it), and n,
the heaviest cell of all, pushes each rung one column up.

### 5.2 What a proof must do, and why depth is the hypothesis

L2 and L4 prove a one-rung ladder: n takes the hub from (c, 1), which falls back on its half, a move, and the proof
puts that half (or more) back in game n − 1 by re-payments inside column 1, with the re-payment lemma's "factor of its
own" supplied by cleanliness (9r > 8p keeps 3r for 6r alone).  A k-rung ladder needs the same in k columns and, in
between, the hub transfers: in game n − 1 the cell (6, 1) must be able to take the hub 6 back from (6, 2) when 6P₁ is
restored, and (4, 2) the hub 4 from (4, 3).  Each transfer is a re-payment whose acyclicity is a closed-walk argument
of L4's kind, and cleanliness to the ladder's depth is exactly what keeps every factor the transfers touch private to
its column.  So the hypothesis of a 16P theorem is "clean to depth 3", of a 24P or 20P theorem "clean to depth 2", of
a 40P theorem "clean to depth 1" (41P₁ > 40P), and the case split is over which cells of the window the optimal set S
of n holds and which factor each pays with.  Nothing in the technique is new; the length is.  For 16P a hand proof
would run three to four times L4, and the families beyond it (20P with its several patterns) are worse.

### 5.3 The finite configuration (a specification, not done here)

For a cofactor c and depth d the window is the cells (k, j), k ≤ c, 0 ≤ j ≤ d, with the hubs k ≤ c and the column
factors (k/q, j).  The following is the same problem for every prime P with the window clean to depth d, so a program
can enumerate it once per cofactor:

1. *States.*  A subset W of the window's cells (those in S), a payment for each member of W from its list (its column
   factors not in W, or its hub), and for each hub k ≤ c one of three marks: paid by a window cell, held outside the
   window (by a cell of some other column, or a move), or free.  F10 fixes the layer-2 cells out of W.
2. *The outside is an oracle with one promise.*  Members outside the window keep their payments.  A hub held outside
   can be taken by a window cell only if freed by n's departure or by a window re-payment; the outside holder's
   precedence edges are unknown, which is where L4's closed-walk arguments enter: a cycle through the outside must be
   shown to be a cycle of S's own scheme.  This step cannot be delegated to the enumeration; it is a lemma to prove
   once, in the form "a re-payment chain that only takes factors released by n or by earlier links of the chain,
   inside one layer instance, creates no cycle unless S's scheme had one".  (That is the common content of L1 part 1,
   L2 case B1, L4 cases B1 and B2; a general version would shorten L4 and make the enumeration sound.)
3. *The check.*  For each state consistent with an optimal S of n (n ∈ W, playable layer instances), the program finds
   a re-payment chain in game n − 1 that puts back cells worth at least the family's law, or reports the state as
   open.  The states where it fails are the theorem's extra hypotheses (as 8p, 8r, 12r, 8t ∈ S are in L4) or its
   counterexamples.

The value: 16P has 44 games past 5060 clean to depth 3 and 48 to depth 2, 20P 20 and 36, 24P 2 and 20, 40P 23
(all), 25P 2 and 18, 21P 17 and 31.  Against this, the chain's CPU in these families is small (16P 2.7%, the rest under
1% each): their worth is past 5060, as rules that settle a best-known game from a proven one.  The parity form (P2)
would cover the same games and more with one computation, so the family proofs are the fallback if the parity form
resists proof, and the first family to do is 16P.

## 6. The exchange law: what is behind it

### 6.1 The law is a statement about one parity-feasible family (proved given U at n − 1 and n, and (♦))

By Theorem A, at an exchange Q\*(n) = Q\*(n − 1) and the family ℒ of promotion sets admissible for the parity bound
is the same for both boards.  Given U at both games, opt(n) − opt(n − 1) = n − x + P\*(n) − P\*(n − 1), and the
succession study's reduction (its Section 1) says the law is P\*(n) = P\*(n − 1).  So:

> **The exchange law says that the heaviest promotion set of n − 1 and the heaviest promotion set of n are equally
> heavy, where both are drawn from one family ℒ, and the two games differ only in which members of ℒ are playable:
> those whose odd cores, with U\*(n − 1), hold x are refused by board n − 1, and those whose odd cores, with U\*(n),
> hold n are refused by board n.**

The one-line reason: a core of U\*(n − 1) ∪ L that does not hold x is a core of U\*(n) ∪ L too, and conversely for
n, since a core is a set of members each of whose listed factors is listed twice within it, and the exchange of two
sources changes no list of any other member (x and n are sources, maximal factors of nothing).  So the only sets ℒ
tells apart are those with a core through x on one board or a core through n on the other.  The law is then: *no
member of ℒ heavier than P\*(n − 1) is playable with U\*(n)*, that is, every L ∈ ℒ with ΣL > P\*(n − 1), which has a
core with U\*(n − 1) (the proof of n − 1), has a core with U\*(n); and that core must pass through n whenever the old
one passed through x.  This is where the 14% of sets that are playable on one board and not the other live (the
succession study's `promsets`): sets whose core holds x, or n, and nothing else.  The law says such sets never sit
above P\*(n − 1).

### 6.2 E1 in this light

E1's boards Y (board n − 1 without x) beat opt(n − 1) − x by 242 to 951, at six of eleven games by playing
S\*(n − 1) with x exchanged for a smaller number y (the shadow of x, a promotion), so with the promotion set
L′ = L + y, worth P\*(n − 1) + y > P\*(n − 1).  On board n − 1 the set U\*(n − 1) ∪ L′ is unplayable, since it would
score opt(n − 1) + y, and every core of it holds x, since without x it is the legal game of Y: L′ is x-bound.  On
board n, E1 measured that the set plus n is unplayable with a core of 13 to 78 members holding n: L′ is n-bound.  So
the E1 detours are instances of the obligation of 6.3 met: x-bound sets above P\*(n − 1) that are n-bound as well.
Nothing local decides it; the core is long because it runs through the hub edges 2q′, 3q′, 2r′, 3r′ that y took
over from x and n's r-side takes back.  (Whether L′ lies in ℒ at all, that is whether U\*(n − 1) ∪ L′ is independent
at both parities, E1 does not say; if it does not, both bounds refuse L′ outright.)

### 6.3 The sharpest testable form found

Call a set L ∈ ℒ *x-bound* when U\*(n − 1) ∪ L is unplayable and every core of it holds x, and *n-bound* likewise.
The law (upper half) is: **every L ∈ ℒ with ΣL > P\*(n − 1) that is x-bound is unplayable with U\*(n) as well**, which
is the one direction the proof of n must supply beyond what the proof of n − 1 refuted; the lower half (measured at
every exchange) is that the heaviest promotion set of n − 1 is not n-bound.  The chain's proofs of exchanges are
cheap only where the near-repeat cut sees this at the node level (4693 in 30 steps); the factor swaps (4899) are
where the x-bound sets are many.  A proof would show that an x-bound set above P\*(n − 1) always has a second core,
one through n, or is beaten by a set that has none.  The previous study's pair lemma and the box's 4-cycle are the
right language, and Section 8's experiment E6 measures, at the cheap exchanges, how many of the refuted sets of n − 1's
proof are x-bound, which is the size of the obligation.

### 6.4 What is settled by Theorem A alone

Nothing about opt, but something about the claim: at every exchange, δ_PB = x exactly, so a best-known game of n at
bk(n − 1) + n − x sits exactly at the parity bound's own prediction, and a lower one is short by a known amount.  All
185 searched exchanges past 5060 are at it (B 4.1).  With the 973 repeats, the exchange games past 5060 are the
games whose scores Theorem A ties to their predecessors' without any U; what U still carries is that the game, not
the bound, moves by n − x.

## 7. The gap

**Proved (Section 3).**  The gap never changes at a prime, at an odd semiprime from 189 on, or, given U at n − 1 and
(♦), at a half-out game; at an exchange it changes by δ − x, which the exchange law says is 0 and the chain never
contradicts.  So gap(5060) = 2,557 is a sum over the chain's relaxations and restrictions alone: by settle.csv, 389
relaxations and 132 restrictions raised it, 315 and 171 lowered it, 20 of the falls at games at the ceiling.

**Judgment (measured, with the mechanism of Section 2).**  B 4.5's judgment is right and can be sharpened.  The gap
is not a sum of loop losses: a clean loop is a circuit the parity bound prices exactly, so it adds nothing.  It is a
running sum of defects: a relaxation or restriction whose cascade meets a configuration that is a circuit in one model
and not the other adds (an odd core n creates, as at 2512) or removes (an odd core n dissolves, as at the 20 ceiling
games) the configuration's worth.  Cleanliness to the cascade's depth is a local test that excludes both, which is
why rise = 0 there and the sign is mixed elsewhere (at depth 0: 489 rises, 442 falls in the chain).  The defects of
unclean steps are not bounded by anything local: 4983's 246 is a regular pattern of eight moves (B 5.3) that the
parity bound does not see at all (δ_PB = 0), and the dearest games are exactly the unclean ones.  A bound on the
defects would need the odd cores of the window's unclean columns, which is the finite check of Section 5.3 applied to
unclean windows: possible per configuration, not in general.

### 10.4 A local proof of δ(5061) ≥ 746: what it is, and what it rests on

In the style of L2 and L4: from any optimal set S of 5061 (given U at 5061, so that S holds U\*(5061): 5019, 14P,
15P, 18P, 20P, 12P, 16P, the far hub holders 5020, 5052, 2826, 4832, 5024, …), T = S − 5061 plus numbers worth at
least 746 is playable in game 5060.  The argument has three parts, of which two are the known technique and one is
new.

1. **Column 0 (finite, L4's kind).**  5061 pays with 7P, 3P or 21.  If 21: the hub 21's holder in S (a cell 21t) lost
   it; in game 5060 put 21t back on the hub or, if 21t ∈ S paying otherwise, put back what it released; the cases
   give at least 746 or a contradiction with S's optimality.  If 3P: 9P ∉ S or pays with the hub 9; put 9P back
   (2169).  If 7P: 14P pays 2P or 14; with 2P, 6P ∉ S or pays with 6 (far: then put 6P's displaced holder back);
   with 6P ∉ S the put-back is 6P = 1446 *provided 18P can release 6P*, that is 18P pays with 9P (then 9P ∉ S, put
   9P back instead) or with the hub 18.  So the one case that does not close at once is: 5061 → 7P, 14P → 2P,
   18P → 6P, 9P ∈ S, and the hub 18 paid by some 18t ∈ S, t ≠ 241.  This is the best-known game's case, and the
   only one the enumeration of Section 5.3 would leave open.
2. **The hub-18 chain (new; the part that serves every c ≥ 18 game).**  In that case the put-back is: 18t gives up
   the hub 18, which 18P takes, and 6P joins paying 2P (+1446); 18t falls back on 6t or 9t, which leaves S if it was
   a move (−6t or −9t), and the column of t re-pays what the departure frees.  So

       δ(5061) ≥ 1446 − R_t,   R_t = the most that column t and the columns coupled to it through hubs can lose
       when 18t loses the hub 18, over every configuration of an optimal S,

   and δ ≥ 746 needs R_t ≤ 700 for every t that an optimal S can use.  Three facts bound where t can be.  (a) In a
   clean column (rows ≤ 20 or 21, the four layer-3 sources saturating 2t, 3t, 5t, 7t) the hub 18 frees nothing
   playable, so an optimal S does not spend it there: t is a prime whose column has fewer saturating sources or more
   candidates, which is a prime far below P (rows above 21) or one of the few above P whose 10t is a source; the
   recorded games use t = 157, 191, 193 and the column −1.  (b) 6t ≤ 700 settles every t ≤ 116 at once.  (c) For the
   remaining t (about thirty primes in 127..281) R_t is the value of hub 18 to column t and its hub neighbors: a
   finite optimization over the column's cells (rows up to 5061/t, candidates up to 2530/t) and the hubs it can
   reach, the same object as E8's window but for an unclean region.  At t = 157 the best-known game says R_t ≥ 700
   (its shuffle reversed), and the parity bound says R_t^PB = 1192: the proof must show that the 492 between them is
   a hub paid twice, for every configuration of the region, not only the recorded one.
3. **The seal (the lemma the first round named, now for an unclean region).**  Part 2's optimization is a proof only
   if the region's cascade cannot leave it: every hub the region touches (18, 27, 29, 30, 32, 16, 12 at t = 157) is
   held by a far cell that is heavier than anything the region offers for it, so no chain crosses out.  That is the
   far-hub lemma of Section 4.4 for the specific hubs of the region, a per-hub check: "the holder of hub h in every
   optimal set is the heaviest h-cell that needs it, and the region's cells are lighter".

So the local proof is: part 1 by hand or by the enumerator (E12), part 2 by a sealed-region search per t (E11's tool,
run for each candidate t with the region free and the rest fixed, *plus* the hub-neighbor columns), part 3 by a
lemma that is new and worth having on its own.  **It is computer-assisted and finite, but it is not a window proof**:
the region is unclean, and that is exactly why the two models part there.  If part 2 returns R_t ≤ 700 for every t
with the seal, the theorem is δ(5061) ≥ 746 given U at 5061, and the best-known game settles 5061.  If part 2
returns R_t > 700 at some t with a playable configuration, that configuration is a better game and 5061 was short.
Either way the run decides the game.

**Does it cover the other 21P exceptions?**  Yes in form: 5943, 6153 (hub 18 to 18·191, 18·193) and 6573, 6657 (hub
18 to column −1, as at 3297 and 3507) have the same parts 1 and 2 with their own t and region; each needs its own
region solve, from a best-known predecessor rather than a proven one, so the result there is "optimal if n − 1 is"
plus the seal.  And 3297 and 3507, proven, are the test of the method: part 2 must return R_t = 540 and 609 there
(the chain's values), which checks the tool before 5061.

**What it says about c ≥ 18 in general.**  Every family from 18 on has a hub (18, 20, or both) that n's arrival
pushes out of column 0 or column 1, and the family law then has two regimes read in game n − 1: the hub is in the
window (a regime-(ii) step, δ = (the half-like cell) − R) or it is not (regime (i), a single-column cascade with a
fixed δ, provable by the enumerator).  R is the value of the pushed-out hub to the rest of the board, a far,
unclean-region quantity; the parity bound's R is at least as large wherever the region has an odd core the bound can
use, and the gap rises by the difference.  So for c ≥ 18: (i) the regime-(i) law is the provable, settling rule
(21P: 34 of the 39 games past 5060; the hub-18 holder of game n − 1 is its test); (ii) regime-(ii) games are settled
only by a region solve with a seal, game by game, which is cheap but needs the seal lemma to be a proof; (iii) the
parity form is not to be trusted there without the seal, since it is exactly where the bound finds hubs to pay twice.
The seal lemma is the one thing that would serve all of them; its statement is a property of hubs and the heaviest
cells, and E14 extended to the far hubs (which cells hold hubs 12..32 in every heaviest set of 5060 and 5061, and
whether the region's cells have circuits through them) is its test.

## 11. What the experiments settle about the principle and its proof

### 11.1 The principle, sharpened (measured, now at the level of sets)

The first round conjectured (P3) that in a clean window the two models take the same augmenting path.  E5 shows it at
the level of sets: at all seven rise-0 games the parity bound's change from n − 1 to n is the game's (D, A) cell for
cell on the whole board, and forced (every heaviest set of n − 1 holds D and avoids A, every heaviest set of n holds
A and n and avoids D), and the defect of n − 1, the pair of parity-only and recorded-only members, passes to n
unchanged.  E8 shows the other half of P3 at the level of games: with everything outside columns 0..3 fixed as the
committed set of n − 1, the window alone reaches opt(n) and the committed set of n at all 31 clean 16P and 20P games,
and the depth it needs is the ladder's (3 for 16P, 2 for 20P), measured without the sets of n.  E7 shows why the
models can agree inside the window: for c ≤ 17 the window instance has no odd core at any depth up to 5, so within
the window a set independent at both parities is playable.  So the principle now reads:

> **(P3′)** In a window clean to the ladder's depth, δ(n) is the window's own optimization (E8), the window's problem
> is the same for the game and for the parity bound when c ≤ 17 (E7), and the parity bound's augmenting path from n
> is the game's cascade (E5).  The gap can change only through a circuit that leaves the window.

The three non-zero rises at depth 1 or 2 are the exceptions that fix the last clause.  At 2512 the window's change is
the cascade, forced, and the extra 98 is an exchange far away (833 → 931) that the cascade *opens*: in every
heaviest set of 2511, 931's circuit at parity 1 (55 members) passes through column 3's cells (4, 3), (9, 3), (12, 3)
and (18, 3), and the cascade's last loss, (4, 3), breaks it.  Row 18 of column 3 is there only because the column is
unclean.  In the game the exchange closes a 14-member odd core far from the window (rows 45..105 of the columns of
19 and 23), so the game cannot follow: the gap rises.  At 688, 1328 and 1460 the parity bound of n − 1 holds hub 4 by

cell of the cascade that leaves the window by a row the clean picture lacks (or, at 1460, by column −1, which the
window of Section 1 does not include; see 11.3).

### 11.2 Can 16P now be proved by the window argument?  Yes, in this form

What a proof needs, and what each experiment supplies:

1. **The finite part (the game side, the family law).**  Section 5.3's enumeration over the window's states, with
   re-payment chains that use only factors released by n or by earlier links and the column's private factors.  E7's
   tool enumerates the window's instance in under a second for (16, 3); E8 says the window reaches opt(n) at the
   recorded sets with depth 3, so the enumeration's depth is right; E7's "no odd core" says no state of the window can
   be independent at both parities and unplayable, so the enumeration can test playability of window states by GF(2)
   rank instead of schemes, which is what makes it a program.  What the enumeration delivers: for every state of the
   window consistent with an optimal set S of n, a chain in game n − 1 worth at least 8P₁ + 6P₂ + 4P₃ − 6P₁ − 4P₂, or
   the states where it fails, which become the theorem's extra hypotheses (as L4's four sources, supplied by U).
2. **The acyclicity lemma (to prove once, by hand).**  A re-payment chain inside one layer instance that takes only
   factors released by n or by earlier links makes no cycle unless S's scheme had one.  It is the common content of
   L1 part 1 and of L4's closed-walk cases; stated once it replaces the per-case walks.  Without it the enumeration's
   chains are not proofs.
3. **The sealing lemma (the parity side, for the equality only).**  No element outside the window has, in a heaviest
   set of n − 1, a circuit through a cell of the ladder, when the ladder's columns are clean.  E5 shows why it is true
   and finite: the circuit through (4, 3) at parity 1 can leave column 3 only through (8, 3), which is in no heaviest
   set (16P₃ pays with it), or through (12, 3), whose vector carries e_{6P₃}, which nothing in a clean column cancels
   (6P₃ is in no heaviest set, and the only other vector with e_{6P₃} is 18P₃'s, absent when the column is clean).
   The same reading gives the lemma for each rung from the column's cells and their coordinates alone, so it is a
   per-cofactor statement a program can check with the window's instance.  E14 below tests it on the data.
4. **Nothing else.**  The far hubs (8, 9, 10, 12, 2) never move at the clean games (Section 5.1), and the chains of
   item 1 never ask them to: a chain takes a hub only when n or an earlier link released it.  So the "far hub lemma"
   of Section 4.4 is not needed for the family law; it was needed only for a general proof across cofactors, which
   this round gives up in favor of one enumeration per cofactor.

With items 1 to 3, the 16P theorem is: *n = 16P ≥ 187, 17P₃ > 16P, and (whatever hypothesis on an optimal set of n
item 1 reports; U supplies it when the named numbers lie in U\*(n)) give opt(n) ≤ opt(n − 1) + n − (8P₁ + 6P₂ + 4P₃
− 6P₁ − 4P₂)*, and rise(16P) = 0 under the same hypotheses with item 3.  Coverage: the 53 clean 16P games of both
ranges (44 past 5060 at depth 3), and the same build does 8P and 12P again as checks of the enumerator against L2 and
L4.  The same enumeration proves 40P at depth 1 (two chain shapes, Section 5.1, both inside column 1 and n's own
column) and should be run next for 20P and 24P at depth 2, where E7 says the window has an odd core, so the
enumeration must keep schemes for playability; E8 says depth 2 suffices for 20P.

## 12. The exchange law: the sharpened target

E6 counts the obligation of Section 6.3 as zero at all eleven cheap exchanges: of 20 to 572,315 refuted sets a game,
none is x-bound.  With symmetry off, the search's exactness argument then says more (E6, item 2): within the root's
candidates, *no member of ℒ worth more than P\*(n − 1) is x-bound* at ten of the eleven games; at 2301 three nogoods
and six conflicts are x-bound, all but one of them n-bound, and the one that is not ({602, 826, 1058, 1113, 1118})
sits in a refuted set that has another core without x.  E1's detours lie outside ℒ at all eleven games (U\*(n − 1)
∪ L′ is dependent at a parity), so they are refused by the parity bound, not by playability.  So the target is:

> **(T) The parity hole law.**  For an exchange step n with shadow x and B = U\*(n − 1) − x: every promotion set L
> with U\*(n − 1) ∪ L independent at both parities and B ∪ L playable on board n − 1 weighs at most P\*(n − 1).

**(T) gives the exchange law's upper half (proved given U at n and (♦) at n − 1 and n).**  An optimal set of n is
U\*(n) ∪ L (U), L is independent at both parities with U\*(n) (P10), hence with U\*(n − 1) (Theorem A: the same
family), and B ∪ L ⊆ U\*(n) ∪ L is playable; so ΣL ≤ P\*(n − 1) by (T), and opt(n) = ΣU\*(n) + ΣL ≤ ΣU\*(n − 1) +
n − x + P\*(n − 1) = opt(n − 1) + n − x.  ∎  The lower half is the game S\*(n − 1) − x + n, measured at every exchange.

(T) is the hole law of the first study with one change: x stays in the parity constraint and leaves only the
playability test, a *ghost*.  The hole law failed by 242 to 951 because its sets could use the hub edges x had
released in any way; (T) lets them use those edges only as the parity bound allows, and E6 says that at ten games no
set above P\*(n − 1) does.  It is a statement about one board and one number, like the hole law, and testable by one
run per game (E9).  What a proof must show: an x-bound set in ℒ, one whose every core with U\*(n − 1) passes through
x, is never heavier than P\*(n − 1).  The pair lemma reduces x's cores to the box's 4-cycle; what is new is that the
parity constraint with x in it bounds how the four hub edges of Q′ ∪ R′ can be used outside the box, which is where
the previous study's Section 3.6 stopped.

**A practical form, exact and conjecture-free (E6, item 1).**  At an exchange the parity bound at every node of the
proof of n − 1 is the parity bound of the same node on board n (Theorem A's argument applies with the node's picks and
bars, since they are candidates and the spans agree), and a conflict or nogood of n − 1's proof refutes the same sets
on board n whenever it has a core without x.  So the proof tree of n − 1 is a proof of n at every node whose cut
transfers, and only the nodes whose conflict is x-bound need a search of their own: at the eleven games, none at ten
and one nogood at 2301.  This is the near-repeat lemma R7 with its conditions replaced by the transfer test
(microseconds a cut), and it would prove the exchanges of a chain of proofs at about the cost of the checks: the 79
searched exchanges of 2..5060 took 10.0% of the chain's CPU.  It needs the proof tree of n − 1 kept (or n − 1's search
re-run with the trace), which the block 5216..5279 can do as it is proven.

## 15. The proof of 5061, and what it teaches

### 15.1 The idea

**What 746 is.**  5061 = 21·241 arrives in a game 5060 whose column 0 plays 6P = 1446 and 9P = 2169 with 18P paying
by the hub 18.  5061 takes 7P, 14P takes 2P, 6P has nothing and leaves, 18P takes 6P and lets the hub 18 go; the hub
lands in a far region, where the best-known game buys back 700 by moving the hubs 18, 27, 29, 30 and 32 among the
columns of 151, 157 and 163.  The parity bound buys back 1192 instead, by putting 8·149 in for 6·241 (E10), and that
exchange, applied to any game, closes a 19-member odd core on the hubs 8, 12, 18, 20, 27 and 30 over the columns of
149, 151, 163, 251, 421 and 631, the same core, column for column and row for row, that the proven games 3297 and
3507 show.  With 5060's set outside, no region up to the columns of all primes 29..1013 holds a better game (E11),
and in the parity model the region of E10 is sealed: its change frees nothing outside it (E14).  So 746 is the
price of a hub's departure settled in a far unclean region, and the question "is 8,057,936 optimal" is the question
whether some game recovers more than 700 there by a configuration that does not have 5060's set outside.

**Why the proof is a finite table, and of what.**  A proof in the style of L2 and L4 is a map: from any playable set
S of 5061 holding 5061, a playable set of 5060 worth ΣS − 5061 + 746 or more.  E12's gluing lemma (refereed in 15.2)
gives such maps without payment schemes: split S into the cells W of a region R (the composite rows of chosen
columns) and the rest O; if a set W′ of cells of board 5060 *dominates* W core for core, then O ∪ W′ is playable,
and δ(5061) ≥ ΣW′ − ΣW + 5061.  The lemma holds for any region whose primes exceed √(n/2) ≈ 50, not only for clean
windows, since the three facts it rests on (a column factor is listed only by its column's cells; a cell lists its
column factors and its hub and nothing else; nothing off the region lists a cell) need only 2P_j² > n.  So the proof
of δ(5061) ≥ 746 is: **for every state W that an optimal S can have on R, a witness W′ worth at least ΣW − 5061 +
746 that dominates it.**  The states are finite once U fixes the sources; the witnesses are searched; a state with
no witness is open, and an open state is either a hypothesis to add, a region to widen, or a better game.

**What is assumed.**  U at 5061, and nothing else beyond the theory: U gives that S holds every source of the region
(the 64 cells of E10's region above 2530 that the proven set of 5060 holds, all of which lie in U\*(5061) = U\*(5060)
+ 5061, a restriction), so that the states differ only in the region's 48 candidate cells (rows ≤ 2530/P_j).  U at
5060 is not needed: the witness is compared with opt(5060) by playability alone.  The gluing lemma uses F10 (n ≥
187) for the prime-row column factors.  Without U the sources would have to be enumerated too, which E12 did for 8P
and 12P (28 and 1,812 states) and which is out of reach here.

### 15.2 The gluing lemma, refereed

**Statement (E12's, generalized to a region).**  Let n ≥ 187 and let R be a set of columns of primes P_j with
2P_j² > n; the *cells* of R are the numbers k·P_j with k composite and k·P_j ≤ n.  Let S be a playable set of n, W
its cells, O = S − W, and W′ a set of cells of board n − 1.  For a set Z of cells, X ⊆ Z nonempty is *column-closed
in Z* when every column factor outside Z that a member of X lists is listed by another member of X; its signature is
R1(X), the rows whose hub exactly one member of X lists, and R2(X), the rows listed by two or more.  If every X
column-closed in W′ has a Y column-closed in W with rows(Y) ⊇ rows(X) and R1(Y) ⊆ R1(X), then O ∪ W′ is playable
on board n − 1, so opt(n − 1) ≥ ΣO + ΣW′ and δ(n) ≥ ΣW′ − ΣW + n.

**My check: the lemma is correct.**  The proof is by P7 (no core in the own instance of O ∪ W′); I verified each step.

1. *O's lists do not change*: a member of O lists maximal factors outside the set; the two sets differ only in cells
   and n, which no member of O lists (fact 3: a cell (k, j)'s multiples are cells of its column or exceed n, since
   k·P_j·q > n for q > P_j; and n is listed by nothing).  Hubs that are moves are in neither set's pool.  ✓
2. *A core K of O ∪ W′ meets W′ in a column-closed set X*: a column factor listed by a member of X is listed by a
   second member of K, which lists a column factor and so is a cell (fact 1), hence in X.  If X is empty, K ⊆ O is a
   core of S by step 1.  ✓
3. *K\* = (K − X) ∪ Y is a core of S*: Y's column factors are covered within Y by its column-closure in W; a hub listed
   by one member of Y is in R1(Y) ⊆ R1(X), so exactly one member of X lists it, and K covers it by a member of K − X,
   whose list is the same in S; a hub listed by two members of Y is covered within Y; a factor listed by a member o
   of K − X is covered in K by another member, which is either in K − X ⊆ K\* or in X, and in the second case it is a
   hub (a non-cell lists no column factor, fact 1), of a row of X, which rows(Y) ⊇ rows(X) covers by a member of Y.
   The condition R1(Y) ⊆ R1(X) is needed exactly: a row in R1(Y) ∩ R2(X) would be covered in K by two members of X,
   neither in K\*.  ✓
4. K\* is a core of the playable S, against P7.  ✓

Three remarks for whoever checks it next.  (a) The "clean column" hypothesis of E12 is not used by the lemma, only
2P_j² > n; cleanliness enters the *enumeration*, where it makes the witness's value a formula valid for all P (the
extreme-ray check).  For a single game such as 5061 the numbers are concrete and no rays are needed.  (b) The lemma
needs only S playable; optimality is used elsewhere (to discard states that a heavier dominated set improves).  (c) A
prime-row column factor is always in the pool by F10 (layer-2 numbers up to n/2 are no moves from 187 on) and because
a layer-2 cell above n/2 is a factor of nothing (2q > c_j).  The lemma and its three facts should be checked by a
reader who has not seen E12; the proof is half a page and the facts are one line each.

### 15.3 The proof of δ(5061) ≥ 746: the computation, sized

**Theorem to establish.**  Let S be an optimal set of 5061 that holds 5061 and the 64 region sources (U).  Then
opt(5061) ≤ opt(5060) + 5061 − 746.  Proof: the table of states and witnesses below, by the gluing lemma.

**The region and its states.**  E10's region, the columns of 149, 151, 157, 163, 241, 251, 421 and 631, has 113
cells, 65 sources (5061 and 64 held by the proven set of 5060) and 48 candidates (`study/size5061_out.txt`).  A
state is a subset of the 48 candidates whose set with the sources and 5061 is coreless (a core of W is a core of S).
Sampling: no uniform subset of the 48 is coreless (0 of 4,000); at the recorded sets' density (13 of 48) one in ten
is; at density 7 of 48 more than half.  So the coreless states are of the order of 10¹⁰, concentrated at low
density: **a flat enumeration of E10's region is out of reach**, in Python or Rust.  The three-column region of the
chain's start, 241, 251 and 157, has 19 candidates and about 1.4·10⁵ coreless states (26% of 2¹⁹), feasible even
in Python; the study's five-column region (36 candidates) about 10⁸ to 10⁹.

**Why the small region is not enough, and what to do instead.**  The best-known game's own state is open in the
three-column region, by hand: its witness restores 6P, and the set X = {18P} is column-closed in the witness (18P's
column factors 6P and 9P are then both members), with rows {18} and R1 = {18}; the lemma asks for a Y in W with a
row-18 cell, column-closed in W, whose hub 18 is the only hub listed once.  In W, 18P lists 6P (no member) and needs
12P, which lists 4P and needs 8P or 20P, which needs 10P or 30P: no such Y in column 0; in the column of 157, 18·157
lists 9·157 (27·157 pays with it) and needs 27·157, whose hub 27 is then listed once, so Y needs a second 27-cell,
27·163, which is in W and lists only its hub.  So Y = {18·157, 27·157, 27·163} exists once the column of 163 is in
the region, and not before.  This is the lemma seeing what the hub 18 does: the witness is valid only where W holds
the structure that pays the hub elsewhere.  The general form is the same: a witness that puts a cell back needs, for
each hub that cell's column then needs, a dominating structure in W, which lies in the columns of the hub's holders.

So the computation is **a region that grows with the open states**: start from the three columns; for each open
state, extend the region by the columns of the hubs the open X's name (the rows of R1(X) not dominated), enumerate
the extension's states, search witnesses again; stop when every state is closed or an open state survives every
extension the hubs name.  The extensions are small: a column of a prime p holds at most 2530/p candidates (9 for
149, 5 for 241, 2 for 421, 1 for 631), and a state's extension into one column is at most 2⁹ states.  Sized: 1.4·10⁵
base states, each with at most a few open X's naming at most a few hubs; each extension multiplies the open states
by at most 512; two rounds of growth give at most about 10⁷ state-extensions, each needing a witness search local to
the change (E12's `--local A,D`, which reproduced the 16P table at (3, 2)).  That is hours in Rust, not in Python.
Optimality prunes further: a state that a heavier dominated W⁺ of board 5061 improves is no state of an optimal S,
and the same lemma proves O ∪ W⁺ playable.

**What each result proves.**  (i) Every state closed with ΣW′ − ΣW + 5061 ≥ 746: the theorem, given U at 5061,
and with the best-known game, opt(5061) = 8,057,936.  (ii) An open state that survives growth and admits a heavier
dominated W⁺ on board 5061: a better game of 5061, found.  (iii) An open state that survives growth with no such
W⁺: a hypothesis for the theorem (as L4's 8t), to be settled by hand or by a further fact (E14's Part B says the
hubs 8, 12, 16 and 20 are held by 8·631, 12·421, 16·313 and 20·251 in every heaviest set and never move; the
analogous game fact, which U gives for the sources and the enumeration gives for their candidates, is what such a
state would need).  The run cannot lie in the direction that matters: a closed state is a proof by the lemma.

**Part 2 over every configuration, closed.**  Section 10.4 asked how the far recovery R_t is bounded for every
configuration of an optimal S, not only 5060's.  The answer is the enumeration itself: the states are every
configuration of the region that an optimal S can have given U, the witnesses are proofs, and the region grows to
whatever columns the hubs name.  No seal lemma is needed as a separate statement: the lemma's domination is the seal,
checked state by state, and E14's parity seal only tells us in advance which columns the growth will ask for (149,
421 and 631 beyond the best-known change's 151, 157, 163, 241, 251).

**A better route?**  None exact and cheaper.  The gap of 5060 (2,557) is inherited by any bound on 5061 that does
not pass through a playable set of 5060, so the parity bound with bars, the frame solver with picks, or any
root-only argument closes at most the 492 and leaves a 2,557-gap search (5060's 213 CPU-hours at best).  The region
solve (E11) is the right experiment and the wrong proof: it fixes the outside, which the lemma must not.  The gluing
route is the one proof whose cost is the region's size and not the board's.
